## 1. What is this Project in Simple Words?

Imagine you are managing the electrical power grid of a state or country. Every second of every day, the amount of electricity generated by power plants **must exactly match** the amount of electricity consumed by homes, offices, factories, and schools.

* If people consume **more** electricity than is generated, voltage collapses, transmission lines trip, and a catastrophic **blackout** occurs.
* If power plants generate **too much** electricity that nobody uses, equipment overheats and millions of dollars in fuel are wasted.

To make things harder, modern grids rely heavily on **renewable energy (solar and wind)**, which fluctuates with the weather. When a cloud passes over a solar farm or the wind suddenly dies down, generation drops rapidly.

### What this project does:
1. It analyzes **8,760 hours (a full year)** of electricity demand, solar power, wind power, and weather data.
2. It uses **Machine Learning** to forecast future electricity demand based on weather, time of day, and calendar patterns.
3. Crucially, it does **not just give a single number** (like *"demand will be 28,000 MW"*). It also calculates an **Uncertainty Prediction Interval** (e.g., *"demand will be between 26,000 MW and 30,000 MW with 90% confidence"*).
4. It is built **strictly using Unit 1 concepts** — meaning simple linear algebra, probability theory, Bayes' rule, and regression — without complicated black-box deep neural networks.

## 2. Why is Uncertainty Quantification (UQ) the Core of this Project?

In typical beginner machine learning projects, students only output a single **point prediction**:
$$\hat{y} = 25,000 \text{ MW}$$

In the real world, a power grid operator will ask: *"What if you are wrong by 3,000 MW? Where will I get backup power?"*

This project solves that by calculating **Uncertainty Bounds**:
$$\left[\hat{y} + e_{0.05}, \quad \hat{y} + e_{0.95}\right]$$

* **Point Prediction:** 25,000 MW
* **Lower Bound (5th percentile of errors):** 22,240 MW
* **Upper Bound (95th percentile of errors):** 27,075 MW

The grid operator now knows:
> *"There is a 90% chance actual demand will stay inside this envelope. I must keep at least 2,075 MW of fast-acting backup generation (called 'spinning reserve') ready in case demand reaches the upper bound."*

## 3. The Dataset: What Data is Used?

The dataset spans all 8,760 hours of a full calendar year (2023) and contains 8 core variables:

| Column Name | Meaning | Physical Range | Why it Matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| `timestamp` | Date and hour of observation | Hourly (Jan 1 to Dec 31) | Captures daily, weekly, and seasonal cycles |
| `electricity_demand_mw` | Total electricity consumed (MW) | 12,330 to 40,031 MW | **Target variable** to predict |
| `temperature_celsius` | Ambient air temperature (°C) | -7.5°C to +39.2°C | Drives heating (cold) and AC (hot) demands |
| `solar_generation_mw` | Solar power generated (MW) | 0 to 7,845 MW | Zero at night; peaks at solar noon |
| `wind_generation_mw` | Wind turbine generation (MW) | 0 to 10,000 MW | Highly volatile, follows Weibull wind distribution |
| `humidity_percent` | Relative humidity (%) | 15% to 99% | Affects human thermal comfort & HVAC load |
| `wind_speed_ms` | Wind speed at hub height (m/s) | 0.5 to 26.0 m/s | Drives wind power via turbine power curve |
| `cloud_cover_percent` | Sky cloudiness fraction (%) | 0% to 100% | Directly blocks solar radiation |

### Real-World Data Preprocessing:
To make the project realistic, the dataset includes deliberate real-world imperfections:
1. **5 Duplicate Rows:** Detected and pruned using timestamp indexing.
2. **25 Missing Sensor Values (NaNs):** Rather than using simple mean imputation (which ruins time-series continuity), we used **time-aware linear interpolation**, maintaining physical realism.
3. **Chronological 80/20 Split:**
   * **Training Set:** First 7,008 hours (January through October)
   * **Test Set:** Last 1,752 hours (late October through December)  
   *Why?* Shuffling time-series data causes **data leakage** (training on the future to predict the past). Chronological splitting simulates real life.

### Deep Dive into the Key Mathematical Concepts

#### 1. Kolmogorov Probability Axioms & The Addition Rule
* **Event $A$:** "High Demand" ($D > 27,714\text{ MW}$, the 75th percentile) $\implies P(A) = 0.2500$
* **Event $B$:** "High Renewable" ($R > 3,760\text{ MW}$, the 75th percentile) $\implies P(B) = 0.2500$
* **Joint Event:** $P(A \cap B) = 0.0626$ (Both demand and renewable are high)
* **General Addition Rule:**
  $$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B) = 0.2500 + 0.2500 - 0.0626 = 0.4374$$
* **Empirical Check on Data:** Exactly **0.4374** (Difference = $0.000000$, proving the axiom holds!).

#### 2. Discrete Random Variables (Converting Continuous Load into States)
We bin continuous demand into 3 discrete operational states:
* **State 0 (Low Demand):** Below 23,061 MW ($P(X=0) = 0.3333$)
* **State 1 (Normal Demand):** Between 23,061 MW and 26,867 MW ($P(X=1) = 0.3333$)
* **State 2 (High Demand):** Above 26,867 MW ($P(X=2) = 0.3333$)
* **Discrete Expectation:** $E[X] = \sum k \cdot P(X=k) = 0(1/3) + 1(1/3) + 2(1/3) = 1.0000$
* **Discrete Variance:** $\text{Var}(X) = \sum (k - E[X])^2 P(X=k) = 0.6667$

#### 3. Bayes' Rule: Updating Risk Under Extreme Weather
Bayes' theorem updates our belief when new evidence is observed:
$$P(\text{High Demand} \mid \text{High Temp}) = \frac{P(\text{High Temp} \mid \text{High Demand}) \cdot P(\text{High Demand})}{P(\text{High Temp})}$$
* **Prior $P(\text{High Demand})$:** $0.2500$ (Base risk is 25%)
* **Likelihood $P(\text{High Temp} \mid \text{High Demand})$:** $0.3575$
* **Evidence $P(\text{High Temp})$:** $0.1997$
* **Posterior:**
  $$\frac{0.3575 \times 0.2500}{0.1997} = \mathbf{0.4477 \quad (44.77\%)}$$
* **Viva Takeaway:** When ambient temperature crosses into the top 20% (> 25°C), the risk of high demand jumps from 25% to nearly 45% (a **1.79× risk increase**).

#### 4. The Independence Paradox: Zero Correlation vs. Independence
> **Examiner's Favorite Question:** *"If two variables have zero correlation, are they independent?"*  
> **Answer:** **No!**

In our dataset:
* Temperature and Demand have a Pearson correlation of $r = -0.0484$ (almost zero!).
* But testing independence shows:
  $$P(A \cap B) = 0.0938 \quad \ne \quad P(A) \cdot P(B) = 0.0625$$
* **Why?** Pearson correlation only measures *straight-line* linear relationships. Because temperature has a **symmetric U-curve** (cold weather increases heating demand; hot weather increases cooling demand), the positive and negative slopes cancel each other out linearly, even though demand is heavily dependent on temperature!

#### 5. Polynomial Curve Fitting & The Thermodynamic U-Curve
* **Degree 1 (Straight line):** Train $R^2 = 0.0014$, Test RMSE = 3,593 MW. Completely fails because it cannot bend. (**Underfitting / High Bias**)
* **Degree 2 (Quadratic parabola):** Train $R^2 = 0.3128$. Successfully captures the U-curve minimum around 18°C–22°C.
* **Degree 3 (Cubic curve):** Train $R^2 = 0.3377$.

#### 6. Supervised Multivariate Regression (The Breakthrough)
Temperature alone only explains ~33% of demand. To predict accurately, we built a multivariate model with **domain-guided feature engineering**:
* Temperature & Temperature Squared ($T^2$ captures heating/cooling)
* Solar Generation & Wind Generation
* Hour of Day, Day of Week, Month, Weekend Flag
* Cyclic Hour Encodings: $\sin(2\pi H/24)$ and $\cos(2\pi H/24)$ (capturing the 24-hour daily human rhythm)

**Results:**
* Baseline Linear Model: Test $R^2 = -0.57$ (Fails on winter test data)
* **Our Feature-Engineered Model:** **Test $R^2 = 0.8092$**, **Test MAE = 1,332 MW** (Excellent accuracy!)

#### 7. Unsupervised Learning (K-Means Clustering)
Without looking at any labels, K-Means ($K=4$) grouped the hours into 4 distinct real-world power grid operational regimes:
1. **Winter Heating Peak (36.9%):** Cold temps (8.1°C), high demand (27,063 MW), low solar.
2. **Off-Peak Baseload / Night (27.0%):** Night hours, low demand (19,575 MW), low renewables.
3. **Summer Cooling Peak (25.8%):** Hot temps (25.8°C), high demand (26,932 MW), high solar (3,060 MW).
4. **Windy Transition (10.3%):** Moderate demand (23,197 MW), very high wind (7,736 MW).

#### 8. Uncertainty Quantification & Prediction Intervals
We compute the prediction residuals (errors):
$$\varepsilon_i = y_i^{\text{actual}} - \hat{y}_i^{\text{predicted}}$$
* Mean error: $-244\text{ MW}$ (negligible bias)
* Error Standard Deviation ($\sigma_\varepsilon$): $1,551\text{ MW}$
* 5th percentile error ($e_{0.05}$): $-2,760\text{ MW}$
* 95th percentile error ($e_{0.95}$): $+2,075\text{ MW}$

**The 90% Empirical Prediction Interval:**
$$[\hat{y} - 2,760\text{ MW}, \quad \hat{y} + 2,075\text{ MW}]$$
When tested across 1,752 unseen test hours, **89.95%** of actual observations fell inside this interval (almost exactly matching the nominal 90% target!).
